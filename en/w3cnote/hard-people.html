Most people's level of effort is so low that it doesn't even come down to competing on talent

Categories Miscellaneous

In the kingdom of the blind, a person with one eye is qualified to be king

Over the past three months or so, the weekly Saturday training course for psychological counselors became one of the most important and happiest things in my week. Because of the National Day holiday, the training center didn't schedule any classes from mid-September to mid-October, so having gotten used to going to class every Saturday, I actually felt quite unaccustomed during the holiday.

Before the holiday, I went to another campus to attend a class. That class was a bit ahead of my class in terms of course progress. During the break, I heard the head teacher, whose hair was already half white, speaking, and the students were eagerly passing around a report card. Only then did I realize they had already taken the introductory exam. The head teacher said to the students, "Before the exam, I predicted that those who came to class every time would pass, many of those who came every time and sat in the front row would get excellent scores, and those who often skipped class, left early, or arrived late would surely include many who failed. After the exam, when the results came out, they were exactly as I predicted." I sat below listening and thought: Teacher, isn't this nonsense? This isn't prediction, it's a universal law. If you often skip class and still get an excellent score on the exam, unless you're a genius and already knew the material in class. Usually, when the exam comes, those who often skip class cram at the last minute, memorize by rote, and barely pass. If you really got an excellent score, either you secretly worked hard outside class, or the test was too easy.

Recently I've been thinking about a question: What causes the differences between people?

My class starts at 8:30 every Saturday morning. In the first month, I always rushed into the classroom just in time, feeling anxious and uneasy, but every time I entered the classroom I was quite pleased, because no more than 20 students were on time. I always belonged to that punctual minority; most people came after 9 o'clock. Later, I gradually adapted and was able to arrive 6 or 7 minutes early, but I found that among the more than 50 students in the class, the number who could attend class on time was even smaller, only about 10. A male classmate impressed me because he was the earliest student to arrive, but he said he was only 15 minutes early. After three months of classes, I was even more pleased, because I found that fewer than 10 students came to class every week without being late or leaving early, and I was one of them. In other words, in three months, of the more than 50 students in the class, more than 40 could not attend class and leave on time every week. This discovery surprised me greatly.Suppose this is a competition of classroom discipline. As long as you attend class and leave on time every week, you can defeat the other 40 competitors.


I know a young guy born in 1992. He graduated from an ordinary second-tier college. Upon graduation, he interned at a certain organization with six other people. After two months, only he was kept. I asked why, saying, "What do you have that's stronger than others?" He said, "I'm not sure either." I kept pressing: "Think again." He said, "Because only I arrived at work on time." I didn't believe it. He added, "Then it's that I wrote emails with more correct formatting and fewer typos, making them easier to understand." This time I believed him, because I deeply experienced this myself. Since I started using Douban, I've read at least a thousand consultation emails. Emails with correct writing format, clear paragraphs, no typos, and organized narration are rare—only a handful. Most are one paragraph from start to finish, with chaotic punctuation, unclear expression, vague topics, and two typos in three lines... often leaving me dizzy. Because I've seen too many messy emails, occasionally when I see a correctly formatted and clearly expressed email, I get so excited I want to cry, and I quickly think about how to reply. If you were me, which kind of email would you be more willing to reply to? Of course, the ones that look more comfortable.

I believe many people's high school Chinese teachers taught them how to get 5 extra points in the college entrance examination. My teacher said this: Keep the paper clean, don't scribble too much, just draw two lines if you're wrong. When writing the essay, write neatly, paragraph clearly, and make the whole exam paper look clean and fresh—you can get five more impression points. He added: Imagine you are the teacher grading papers, in the scorching heat of June and July, seeing dirty papers and sloppy handwriting, how would you feel?

I myself have organized several activities and needed to send text messages to activity members. The people who made an impression on me at once were those with good text-message manners. What are good text-message manners? It's someone who replies promptly every time they receive a message, quickly responding with "Received," "Okay, thanks." Compared to those who stay silent for a long time after receiving a message, and you don't know whether they saw it, I prefer people with good text-message manners. When in contact with them, I pay attention to my own text-message manners and learn things from them that I didn't understand before.

After publishing two books, especially after publishing "Take It Slow, Everything Will Be in Time," I was often asked a question that annoyed me to no end. Many people saw me as if I were Ultraman, thinking I was magical: "How do you manage to write a book while working?" At first I answered honestly: "It's nothing. Sometimes when I have nothing to do after work, I sit and write articles for an hour or two, and slowly it became a book." After I said that, many people still didn't believe it, thinking I must have tied my hair to a beam and pricked my thigh with an awl, slogging through a painful 81 months before writing a book. Later, when people asked again, I could only say: I worked extremely, extremely, extremely hard when writing the book!

After all this talk, let me summarize in one sentence:With most people's level of effort being so low, it doesn't even come down to competing on talent.It is said that there was a survey experiment asking: If you're not good at math, can you learn things like computer programming well? More than 70 percent of people chose "no." Then a professor summed it up with that sentence.Many people always think that what they compete with others is endurance, talent, hard work and diligence, or being a desperate study machine. In fact, it's just a little bit of conscientiousness, a little bit of attention to detail, a little bit of doing one's duty—not even enough to count as diligence. Around you, there are simply too many lazy people. As long as you achieve basic industriousness, you can become rich; in your world, most people are blind. If you have just one eye, you are qualified to be king.

I have received many complaining emails. Some complain that their work compensation is too low, some complain that their work content is trivial, some complain about social injustice, some complain about a poor family environment, some complain that their educational background isn't high enough, some complain that they're not doing what they like, and some, when writing these complaining emails, have two typos in three lines, a paragraph of two thousand characters, and add a note saying "Urgent." Among these people, how many have done what they should have done and done it well? I think not many. A person can be forever dissatisfied with the status quo, but should be content with the status quo. Simply put, whatever you do, you should be dedicated, keep to your own duty, and do what needs to be done well—rather than not even doing the most basic effort, yet using illusory equality, magnificent dreams, social injustice, or lack of talent as excuses for not succeeding.

How can a person stand out from competition? In fact, it's very simple. The things he needs to do are mostly small things, even some very basic duties.A person shows himself through details and small things.The differences between people mostly lie in details and simple small matters.All abilities to do great things also grow from small things.Sometimes success isn't that complicated. If you do the small things that others haven't done or have ignored, you win. The so-called "details determine success or failure" is an eternal truth.As a student, be on time for class and leave on time, and complete assignments on time. At a graduation interview, if you can't afford brand-name clothes, dress yourself clean and neat, and be punctual for the appointment. When you start working, when writing emails, pay attention to writing format, clear paragraphs, and clear expression. When the boss assigns things, ask a few more questions and clarify before acting; when the boss asks you to report on your work, respond promptly. At work, play fewer games, browse Taobao less, and think more about how to do things well. After work, watch a few fewer TV series, sleep a little less, and read a few more books. On weekends, while everyone is eating, drinking, and sleeping in, go for a run in the park and exercise.Do these things, and your opportunities will naturally increase, and your fate will slowly change.

Don't look down on small things. Life is inherently a collection of small things. Persist in doing each small thing well, and you'll live your life well and change your own destiny. If you go a step further—besides doing your duty, you can also take every small thing to the extreme, like Cook Ding butchering the ox—what would happen? There's a line in "Soldiers' Sortie": Every small thing he did was like grabbing a life-saving straw. In the end, you realize that what he had his arms around was already a towering tree.
